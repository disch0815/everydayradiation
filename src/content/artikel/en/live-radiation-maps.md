Environmental radioactivity is monitored around the clock across much of the world — and nearly all the data is public. If you want to know how much radiation there is outside your door right now, you just need the right maps.

## Official networks

- **USA — RadNet:** the EPA's [RadNet network](https://www.epa.gov/radnet) runs monitors across all 50 states and publishes near-real-time air readings.
- **Europe — EURDEP:** the EU Commission's [European map](https://remap.jrc.ec.europa.eu/Simple.aspx) pools some 5,500 stations from 39 countries.
- **Switzerland — NADAM:** the [National Emergency Operations Centre](https://www.naz.ch/aktuell/messwerte.html) maps current ambient dose rates nationwide.
- **Germany — ODL-Info:** the federal radiation office shows readings from about 1,700 stations at [odlinfo.bfs.de](https://odlinfo.bfs.de/ODL/DE/home/home_node.html).

## Citizen science: the unofficial net

After the 2011 Fukushima accident, volunteer-run global networks emerged: [Safecast](https://map.safecast.org/) aggregates measurement drives by volunteers, [uRADMonitor](https://www.uradmonitor.com/) operates a real-time sensor network. These data aren't officially validated — but they're a fascinating complement and proof that measuring radiation is no secret art.

## What's "normal," anyway?

The maps show the **ambient dose rate**, usually in microsieverts per hour (µSv/h). Typical values:

| Reading | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 0.05–0.2 µSv/h | Normal range in lowlands |
| 0.2–0.5 µSv/h | Normal in mountain regions (more cosmic radiation, uranium-bearing rock) |
| brief spikes | Often just rain: it washes natural radon decay products out of the air |

Fluctuations within these ranges are everyday life, not a warning sign. Mountain readings beat lowland readings everywhere — someone living in Denver "collects" measurably more per year than someone in Miami, with no detectable health consequence. How to put such numbers in context is covered in [Millisieverts explained](/en/articles/millisievert-explained).
