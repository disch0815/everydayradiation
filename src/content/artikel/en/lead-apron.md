If your dentist no longer drapes you in a lead apron, you're not witnessing negligence — you're watching science in action. In 2024 the **American Dental Association formally recommended discontinuing lead aprons and thyroid collars for dental X-rays**, following the lead of the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM). What sounds paradoxical at first has solid reasons.

## Why the apron does less than everyone assumed

1. **Modern equipment collimates precisely.** Today's beams hit only the target area — in dental imaging, the jaw. Organs outside that area receive almost nothing.
2. **The scatter that matters arises inside the body.** The little radiation that reaches other organs is mostly scattered **internally** — and an apron lying on the outside cannot shield against radiation traveling inside the body.
3. **The apron can actually cause harm.** If it drifts into the image, the exposure must be repeated — doubling the dose. And with automatic exposure control, a badly placed shield can push the machine to increase output.

The AAPM laid out these arguments in its influential 2019 position statement (the CARES initiative); the ADA followed in 2024, and European professional bodies have taken the same line.

## Does that mean no protection at all?

Not quite. What remains is the protection that actually works: **a justified exam, the smallest useful field of view, and correct settings** — generating less radiation instead of trying to catch it afterwards. Children are imaged especially conservatively under the "Image Gently" principle. And where shielding makes sense in an individual case, it can still be used — that's a clinical judgment based on current guidance, not a ritual.

## What to take away as a patient

If no apron is offered, your practice is likely following the current standard of care — not cutting corners. The more useful question than "where's the apron?" is: "what will this image change about my treatment?"
