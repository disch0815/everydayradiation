At cruising altitude the air is thin — including as a radiation shield. **Cosmic radiation** from space is normally absorbed by Earth's magnetic field and the thick blanket of atmosphere. At 33,000–39,000 feet, much of that protection is missing: the dose rate up there is roughly **100 times higher** than at sea level.

## The numbers for a transatlantic flight

On a flight from Zurich or Frankfurt to New York (about 8 hours), you pick up — depending on route, altitude, and solar activity — roughly **0.04–0.06 millisieverts (mSv)**. Round trip: about **0.1 mSv**, or 10 millirem.

What does that mean in practice? Comparison makes it tangible:

| Activity | Dose (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| Europe–New York flight (one way) | 0.05 mSv |
| Single dental X-ray | 0.005 mSv |
| Dental cone-beam CT (CBCT) | 0.05–0.2 mSv |
| Average annual dose, U.S. resident | ~6 mSv |

A transatlantic flight is roughly equivalent to a small [dental CBCT scan](/en/articles/dental-x-ray-dose) — or a few days of ordinary background radiation. Want the exact number for your route? The free [flight dose calculator at kurse.dirkschulze.ch](https://kurse.dirkschulze.ch/flugdosis/) computes the dose for specific flights and compares it directly with a CBCT scan.

## Why the route matters

Earth's magnetic field deflects charged particles toward the poles. That means:

- **Polar routes** (e.g., Europe–U.S. West Coast over Greenland) collect more dose than equatorial ones.
- **Higher means more:** the dose rate at 39,000 feet exceeds that at 30,000 feet.
- **Solar activity:** after strong solar flares, radiation can spike briefly; in extreme events airlines reroute or fly lower.

## Should you worry?

For vacation and business travelers: no. Even ten long-haul flights a year add up to about 0.5 mSv — a fraction of the natural annual dose. The math only changes for people who spend hundreds of hours a year in the air for a living — more on that in [Frequent flyers and crew](/en/articles/frequent-flyers-and-crew).
