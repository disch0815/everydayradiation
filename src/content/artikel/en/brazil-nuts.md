It sounds like clickbait, but it's settled science: **Brazil nuts are among the most radioactive foods on Earth.** The reason isn't a scandal or contamination — it's botany.

## Why Brazil nuts, of all things?

The Brazil nut tree grows in the Amazon and has an extraordinarily deep, wide-spreading root system. Along with minerals like barium it also takes up **radium** — chemically very similar and naturally present in soil — and concentrates it in its seeds: the nuts. According to Germany's Federal Office for Radiation Protection (BfS), Brazil nuts can contain up to **1,000 times the radioactivity** of other plant foods; the U.S. EPA features them in its RadTown series for the same reason.

## How much dose does that add?

The BfS has done the math: eating **two Brazil nuts a day** adds roughly **0.16 millisieverts (mSv)** — about 16 millirem — to your annual effective dose. For comparison:

| Source | Dose (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| 2 Brazil nuts daily, for a year | 0.16 mSv |
| One transatlantic flight (one way) | 0.05 mSv |
| Single dental X-ray | 0.005 mSv |
| Average annual dose, U.S. resident (natural + medical) | ~6 mSv |

The occasional handful from the snack mix simply doesn't register. Even daily consumption stays within a few percent of the background radiation you're exposed to anyway.

## Selenium: the real reason for moderation

Interestingly, radioactivity isn't why nutrition experts advise moderation — **selenium** is. Brazil nuts are extremely rich in it; a few nuts cover your daily requirement, and chronically high amounts can lead to selenium excess. The classic advice of one to two nuts per day is right twice over.

## Bottom line

Brazil nuts are the perfect example of how "radioactive" doesn't automatically mean "dangerous." Natural radioactivity is in virtually every food — Brazil nuts just have conspicuously more. Enjoyed in normal amounts, they pose no problem from either the radiation or the selenium. For the bigger picture, see [How radioactive is our food?](/en/articles/radioactivity-in-food)
